Is it a cold, flu or Covid? How to tell the difference

  • Published
    3 October 2025
Updated 8 hours ago

There are lots of coughs and sneezes doing the rounds at the moment, so how do you know if it might be covid or flu rather than a cold?

Here's how to spot the difference and hopefully avoid the worst this winter.

What is flu?

NHS leaders are already warning that the UK is seeing a "horrible spike" in flu, with hospital cases higher than usual for the time of year.

Flu has hit a bit earlier than normal.

And we are seeing a 'drifted' strain, external, say experts, meaning one that is slightly different to what was anticipated when this year's vaccine was being made.

But vaccines still work against the virus that is constantly changing or mutating, they say. The vaccines are updated each year to ensure they continue to be a good match.

So far this season, there's a mix of two flu A subtypes, H1N1 and H3N2, circulating in England, says the UKHSA, external.

The flu vaccine is designed to protect against all three main types of flu - influenza A(H1N1), A(H3N2) and influenza B. Whichever ends up dominating this winter, or if they all circulate at similar levels throughout, the vaccine should offer broad protection.

Some people can get a free NHS flu vaccine, external. Anyone else who wants one can buy it at a pharmacy.

Flu symptoms tend to start more suddenly than a cold or Covid, and are usually more severe.

Common flu symptoms include a fever, aching muscles and chills.

Is it a cold? Does cold weather cause it?

Cold symptoms tend to be milder and come on more gradually than Covid or flu.

It might include a sore throat, as well as coughing and sneezing.

Research on the extent to which cold weather impacts our immune systems remains unclear.

But darker days mean we typically make a beeline for warm, cosy, indoor spaces.

And this environment is perfect for viruses.

Schools, nursery and even some workplaces can be like a petri dish with lots of viruses circulating. The Christmas season raises the risks as more people socialise indoors.

The same applies to freshers in university halls - where mixing together spreads germs, while heavy drinking and partying further weaken the immune system.

Could it be Covid?

It is still possible to catch Covid. Common symptoms include a high temperature and a cough, as well as feeling exhausted.

One key Covid-specific identifier can be a loss of smell or taste.

While no one is told to isolate any more, the advice is try to stay at home and avoid contact with other people if you or your child have symptoms.

Most people do not need to take a Covid test if they have symptoms, but they are available to buy in chemists and, while not 100% accurate, they can help confirm or rule out infection.

Colds

  • Symptoms appear gradually

  • Mostly affect nose and throat

  • Early sign - pressure in your ears

  • Chestier, mucus cough

Flu

  • Comes on suddenly

  • Feel wiped out

  • Fever, muscle aches, exhaustion

  • Need bed rest

  • Dry cough

Covid

  • Typical flu symptoms

  • Loss of taste or smell

  • Diarrhoea or tummy upset

Many of the symptoms between cold, flu and Covid, overlap.

But there are some clues that might help you identify the exact culprit.

If a cold's coming on, then it often does so gradually.

It's going to affect your nose and the back of your throat, while some will get a tickling at the back of the mouth.

Another early warning sign can be a build-up of pressure in the ear.

If the virus spreads further, it can reach your lungs and cause a pesky cough.

Mostly however, these symptoms don't stop us from carrying on as normal.

The same can't be said for flu, which typically adds aches, fevers and muscle weakness into the mix.

Flu doesn't always mean you have to go to bed, but it might make you feel that way.

Since the pandemic, Covid has complicated things further, with similar symptoms to flu.

The recommendation is to stay home and rest and recover.

However, if you have underlying conditions, experience breathlessness or find symptoms don't ease after three weeks, then you shouldn't hesitate to seek medical advice.

Can I help make myself better?

Our bodies naturally fight viruses and infection, but we can also give it a helping hand with the right medication.

  • Paracetamol: Provided you're okay to take it, this or ibuprofen is the first port of call. Both are really good for helping to bring the fever down and softening any pain that you might have. But remember that lots of cough and cold mixtures include traces of paracetamol, so make sure you're not accidentally taking too much

  • Vitamin C: This is often thought to help defend against colds. But there's not a huge amount of evidence for this. Unless you're deficient, focusing on a healthy, balanced diet is far more important

  • Vitamin D: A top-up of vitamin D in colder months is recommended by the NHS, because let's face it, we don't usually get much sunlight at this time of year in the UK

  • Decongestant spray: Sure, they feel great and provide instant relief, but using sprays too often can cause rebound congestion- when your nose becomes dependent and swells without a fix. I suggest using them for no more than four of five days

  • Chicken soup: There's little evidence of the dish fighting viruses directly, but its heat can help warm the back of your throat and soothe some of those symptoms. It's also a good way to take on extra fluids, with hydration vital as you battle infection.
