Villagers to vote on 'leaving UK' over plans to house migrants at RAF base

ByGeorge King
Suffolk
  • Published
    5 hours ago

Villagers will head to the polls on Saturday to decide whether or not to symbolically leave the UK over plans to house migrants at a former military base.

RAF Barnham, between Bury St Edmunds, Suffolk, and Thetford, Norfolk, has been earmarked by the government as a potential base for up to 931 migrants.

The proposals have not gone down well with locals and far-right groups alike, sparking several anti-asylum demonstrations since the summer.

One way or another, residents are now poised to make their voices heard by taking part in a referendum to decide whether to symbolically secede from the country or remain.

Here is everything you need to know about the controversy that has thrust a tiny, otherwise unassuming village into the national spotlight.

What are the government's plans?

The Home Office first floated the idea of using three Ministry of Defence (MoD) sites to house thousands of asylum seekers in June.

Facilities in Bicester in Oxfordshire, Linton-on-Ouse in North Yorkshire, and Barnham in Suffolk were identified to help move people out of hotels.

As of March this year, 20,885 (21%) asylum seekers were living in hotels, while 72,768 (75%) were in other accommodation as they awaited decisions.

The Home Office initially sought to house 1,250 migrants at RAF Barnham, but that number has since been reduced to just shy of 1,000.

According to the government, they would be "single adult males" aged between 18 and 65 years old who could be permitted to leave the site.

If planning permission is granted and the proposals go ahead, "a specialist and experienced provider of security services" would be "permanently on site 24/7".

It is also hoped that there will be enough "essential services" to reduce "the need for asylum seekers to leave the site".

The Home Office, which could use a fast-track planning route to bypass local council permission, is said to have awarded a £3.4m contract for the project to Kier.

The construction giant has been hired to design the camp at the former nuclear weapons store, which has most recently been used for police training.

How do residents feel about the proposals?

The site is located just over a mile away from its namesake village, nearly two miles from Thetford, and close to four miles from Elveden by road.

The plans have been met with strong opposition from local councillors and residents, such as Kim Dines, who has lived in the village for 13 years.

She previously said she had been "lying awake at night" wondering what may result from migrants being housed "so close".

"We're a village of roughly 600 people; we shouldn't be living in fear, we shouldn't be worried about what's going to be on our doorstep [and] who's around us," she said.

Ann Houston said the presence of migrants nearby might alter "the atmosphere in the village".

"Barnham is a place where you can leave your doors open at the moment, and that might change," she said.

Concerns have also been raised about the government's commitment to spend £3.4m on drawing up the scheme before a final decision has been made.

Helen Beck, chairwoman of the Barnham Residents Association, said: "At the moment they don't even know whether it can legally go ahead - hugely disappointing."

John Bauer, chairman of Barnham Parish Council, said leading the campaign against the plans was ruining his retirement.

"I retired at the end of last year with the expectation I would be able to do a lot of the things that I had been looking forward to doing for a while," he said.

"Unfortunately, that hasn't happened. It is demanding and exhausting, and it is not necessarily what I would have chosen to do in [my retirement]."

What would leaving the UK actually mean for Barnham?

In many respects, voting to break away from the UK would very much act as a symbolic gesture and a way for the village to demonstrate its strength of feeling.

That is because there is no legal mechanism allowing the likes of Barnham to declare independence from the UK.

Has this been done before?

Yes, quite recently, in fact – and for similar reasons.

In September, villagers in Piddington, near Bicester, in Oxfordshire voted to become independent of the UK.

Like in Barnham, it was held in protest against plans to house up to 1,256 male asylum seekers at nearby MoD Bicester.

On that occasion, out of 312 votes cast, 285 people voted to leave, 26 voted no, and one ballot paper was spoiled.

When and where is the referendum taking place and who can vote?

Voting is taking place at Barnham Village Hall from 10:00 GMT to 16:00, before counting gets underway and the result is announced between 17:00 and 18:00.

The council cannot be completely sure how many people will be eligible to vote, due to being unable to use the electoral register, but believes up to 500 could take part.

In the interests of accessibility, some sealed votes will be collected by the council, which has also taken note of those who require help getting to the polling station.

How many people are in asylum accommodation?

An asylum seeker is someone who wants to be in the UK because they say they cannot live safely in their own country due to persecution or violence.

The government must house an asylum seeker if they cannot financially support themselves while their claim is being considered. There were 89,089 people in asylum accommodation as of June 2026.

About 18% of those people, 16,021, were in hotels. These are used when there is not enough shared housing available, such as houses of multiple occupation (HMOs) or former military sites.

The government has pledged to stop the use of hotels by 2029.

More people are in hotels across the south of England than elsewhere in the UK, while HMOs are more common in the north of England and Scotland.

How many people apply for asylum in the UK?

A total of 85,891 people applied for asylum in the UK from July 2025 to June 2026. This was down by 21% on the same period in the previous year.

Most people who arrive in the UK by small boat claim asylum, but they were only 40% of all asylum seekers from July 2025 to June 2026.

The rest include people who arrived by other illegal means or who came to the UK legally and applied for asylum while holding, or just after holding, a valid visa.

How big is the asylum backlog?

It can take years for the government to decide whether someone should be granted asylum.

In June 2026, 40,168 people - across 32,401 asylum applications - were waiting for an initial decision. The number of people awaiting an initial decision was down by 56% on the previous year.

As of June 2026, a further 90,341 refused asylum applications were part of a second backlog, waiting the outcome of an appeal in the courts. This was up by 51% on the previous year.

Together, these initial decision and appeal cases form the government’s total asylum applications backlog, which it has pledged to clear.

Refugee status was granted in about 38% of asylum decisions made by the Home Office from July 2025 to June 2026.

About 39% of asylum appeal cases concluded between January 2026 to December 2026 resulted in a previous refusal decision being overturned.

What does the Home Office say?

The Home Office said it was "listening carefully to the concerns" of residents and that no decision had yet been taken.

A spokesperson said: "The government is working on a fairer distribution of asylum accommodation across the country, with over £1bn saved in costs since the election.

"This includes closing all asylum hotels and moving to alternative accommodation, including former military sites.

"We recognise the strength of feeling in local communities and are listening carefully to the concerns being raised."
