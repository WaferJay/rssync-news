These dead pets were meant to be cremated - then their bodies appeared on a livestream

ByGavin Butler
  • Published
    8 hours ago

The livestream begins.

A man – bald, goateed, wearing red-tinted sunglasses – walks down a cracked back road, guiding the camera towards a cluster of plastic-wrapped objects that spill out of the tall grass.

Names are written on the packages – Brandon; Ira; Lucky; Sweet – and numbers. One says 29kg (4.5 stone). Another says 2.5kg. Through the plastic you can make out what appears to be fur, claws and a flattened ear.

It's a mass dumping, the man explains. "We have about 25 animals here that have been dumped in bags. A couple of cats, some dogs."

"Purebreds," says the woman filming.

Warning: This story contains images some might find upsetting

In the weeks since that video was streamed on Facebook, Chris and Geraldine Taylor, who run animal rescue NGO Luna and Furry Siblings (LAFS), say they have uncovered 15 dumping sites in the suburbs of Bulacan, near the Philippines' capital of Manila.

In total they've unearthed the remains of some 170 dead pets. Belgian Malinois, golden retrievers, pugs, puppies and cats have been found dumped off service roads, sealed in septic tanks, floating in rivers and buried in vacant lots.

Many of these animals are thought to have been sent to cremation businesses in the expectation they would be turned to ash.

Now, many grieving owners are having to confront a distressing reality: their beloved pets may not have received the send-off they paid for. The urn in their home may not contain their pet at all. And they may in fact see their so-called fur child again, jettisoned like common waste on the side of a highway.

One saw the livestream and wrote on Facebook: "I think one of those pets is mine." Below, they attached a photo of a beaming Alsatian.

170 carcasses found

When Chris arrived on that back road three weeks ago, he couldn't have known he was uncorking a scandal that would see at least one business reportedly shut, four people placed under investigation, and a personal invite to sit down with a senator to discuss animal-cruelty laws.

Chris, a former police officer, and his wife are retired, but only formally. For the past three years, they've been running an animal rescue shelter out of their home in San Jose del Monte, 30km (18.6 miles) north-east of central Manila.

On 30 October, he'd driven into town to report a case of animal neglect at the local district office. When he arrived, he was told the official in charge had just left to respond to another complaint: a report of dumped animals on a nearby roadside.

Chris had seen this once before: 14 dogs that were found and soon reburied. This time, he followed the official to the scene and started documenting – taking photos, scanning the bodies for microchips and uploading footage to his Facebook page.

"I figured there's got to be more to this," he explains. "So we put out word: if anybody knows anything, any other dump locations... just report it. Let us know."

In the days that followed, tip-offs started trickling in. More dumpsites emerged.

"The long vacant lot on the left side... that's where the smell comes from," read one message.

"When we ran to this place it smelled of rotting animals," read another, accompanied by a photo of a map with a location circled in red.

By crowdsourcing intel in this way, and collaborating with authorities, LAFS has so far uncovered a total of 15 dumpsites, containing an estimated 170 animals. "Some of them were just bags of bones," Chris says. "Others were liquefied."

LAFS has meticulously documented the crime scenes. Often wearing little more for protection than an N95 mask and rubber gloves, Chris crouches in the underbrush and slices open discarded plastic bags, picking through the contents while Geraldine holds the camera.

Their Facebook videos have racked up more than 30 million views. Commenters ask Chris if he has seen their pet among the bodies, or if he can turn a carcass over so they can check for identifying marks.

Some are successful. On that first night in October, all 25 bodies were returned to their owners.

There's a question which hangs uncomfortably over every dumpsite: who would do something like this?

As more owners came forward, a common denominator emerged. Many said they'd taken the animals to be incinerated at the same place: a crematorium called Ahjay Paws in the city of Bacoor, not far from Manila.

"So I posted: anybody that's had Ahjay's services... these might be your animals," says Chris. "And man, it just flooded in."

'God knows what was inside those urns'

The Philippines has long been a dog-loving nation, but since the Covid-19 pandemic the "fur parenting" community has exploded.

A 2021 study found the country had the highest rate of dog ownership in the Asia-Pacific. Since then, the pet dog population has increased 4.7% year on year, according to another study, exceeding 21 million in 2025.

As the numbers soar, businesses have stepped up to address an inevitable implication: more deaths.

"Cremation here in the Philippines has become quite a big thing with the boom of the pet industry," says Anna Cabrera, director at the Philippine Animal Welfare Society (Paws). "There's even a ceremony where they hold wakes for the pets."

These events offer grieving owners a last chance to see their beloved pets before they're taken to a crematorium and transmuted into an urnful of ashes.

Or not, as the case may be.

Revecca Calling, who lives south of Bulacan in the city of Cavite, says she paid Ahjay Paws 8,500 Philippine pesos (£100; $133) to cremate her dog Olaf in January. The price was calculated according to weight, and included a 30-minute viewing where she could say her final goodbye.

Calling says Ahjay Paws told her they couldn't record the cremation, as it might endanger the staff. They took away Olaf's body, and a few days later handed back a small urn that purportedly contained her cremated remains.

When nine months later Calling heard that dozens of animals' bodies had been found dumped on an empty plot of land, potentially at the behest of a pet cremation business, she was devastated.

"Pet owners entrust these services with something very important: the remains of a beloved family member," she says. "We need to know what happened."

Earlier this month, the Philippines' National Bureau of Investigation (NBI) filed criminal complaints against four individuals in connection with dumped pets. Among them is the owner of Ahjay Paws and two people who ran Bulacan's Pet HPHT Crematory.

According to the NBI, Ahjay Paws had initially arranged to acquire a cremation machine from Pet HPHT – but when it didn't arrive, they opted to send the pet remains to Pet HPHT instead. When the volume of those remains became more than Pet HPHT could handle, that firm paid a third party to bury them.

One interpretation is that the dumping incidents are a result of logistical failures: two businesses that bit off more than they could chew and took desperate measures to make the problem go away.

But there's another theory: that the dead pets could be evidence of a lucrative racket, with people's money taken to incinerate their pets but the animals then being dumped instead.

There is a word for this kind of deception in Philippine law – estafa; where a perpetrator knowingly misleads a victim to financially defraud them – and the NBI say they are investigating it as a possibility in this case.

Two weeks ago, Ahjay Paws' owner denied responsibility for the dumping in San Jose del Monte, according to local media. The company is apparently no longer operating, and the BBC has been unable to contact it.

The BBC has also reached out to Pet HPHT and invited it to respond. On Facebook, the firm said the dumping allegations were "completely false and have no basis".

Another grieving pet owner, Emelia Legada, says she paid Ahjay Paws 10,000 Philippine pesos (£120; $160) to cremate her dog Harvey six months ago.

"I just can't accept the pain of not knowing what might have happened to him," she says. "I really want to confirm whether Harvey was properly cremated, and whether the ashes I received were truly his."

Many are wrestling with the same uncertainty. As Cabrera puts it: "God knows what that was inside those urns."

In response to those anxieties, a veterinarian in nearby Quezon City has started offering complimentary forensic ash testing. For those whose pets have been found among the bodies, other crematoriums have offered to do the job properly, and for free.

The animal welfare society, Paws, is volunteering legal services to help people file complaints with the authorities, and has received more than 150 requests so far. It is also calling for a nationwide audit of all pet cremation businesses.

Animal-cruelty law 'doesn't apply'

On 2 November, Percival Vilar Cendaña of the Philippines' progressive Akbayan Party appeared on national television. He was there to speak about the pet-dumping scandal, which, in the span of a few days, had flared into a national disgrace.

"Our worst fears have been confirmed," he told the Bilyonaryo news channel. "Now we see that these are not isolated incidents. It is true that this is a syndicate that is conducting widespread operations."

Neither the police nor the NBI have confirmed that the dumpings were carried out by a "syndicate", nor that they were "widespread". Many details surrounding the case remain murky. But the hysteria is real – and Cendaña was giving voice to a common sentiment.

"Those who deceived these poor fur parents should be jailed," he said.

At least three individuals have surrendered to the NBI and been charged with violating the city's Code of Sanitation, the Ecological Solid Waste Management Act, and the Sanitation Code of the Philippines.

But many feel this doesn't go far enough, and say it fails to hold anyone liable for the way the animals' remains were treated.

Cabrera says the country's animal-cruelty law doesn't cover the treatment of animals after death. And "there's no crime if there's no law".

The scandal has prompted a groundswell to change that. Lawmakers like Cendaña are pushing for a national commission on animal welfare to ensure their dignity in life and death.

Meanwhile, Cabrera says Paws will seek "moral and exemplary" damages on behalf of aggrieved pet owners.

One woman whose dog was suspected to have been found among the dumped bodies told the BBC she couldn't speak about it because she was "feeling weak".

"I haven't slept for three days from all the crying," she said.

Chris has also been losing sleep, taking calls from grieving pet owners who have watched his videos. "Hundreds of people call me in tears, just sobbing," he says.

"They're in so much pain. And half the time you're telling them: 'you're not going to find your pet. It's been too long; we're not going to be able to identify it.' And it just crushes them."

As well as new laws, Chris wants stricter enforcement of existing rules. He wants to know how he managed to unearth so much in the space of a fortnight, and why people keep coming to him saying they don't trust the police to take their concerns seriously, he says.

The BBC contacted Philippine National Police (PNP) for comment, but didn't hear back. Earlier this month, PNP chief Jose Melencio Nartatez Jr said authorities were determined to hold accountable those who allegedly took advantage of grieving pet owners, saying such exploitation "must be addressed with the full force of the law", according to ABS-CBN.

"Fraud is not measured solely by the amount of money taken, but also by the damage inflicted on the people whose trust has been betrayed," Nartatez said.

"To the affected pet owners, we recognise the pain and distress this incident has caused your families."

Yet the police themselves have come under official scrutiny.

On Monday, the National Police Commission announced an investigation into officers in Cavite and Bulacan after they suggested LAFS might be involved in the pet dumping because they were often first on the scene. The officers did not present evidence for their claims. The investigation also covers reports that officers visited the Taylors' home and took photographs.

Meanwhile, countless grieving pet owners are suspended in purgatory: desperate for closure on what happened to their dogs and cats, and calling on authorities to do more.

"We want the case to be properly investigated and those responsible to be held accountable," says Calling.  "I believe that no secret remains hidden forever."

Legada, for her part, says: "I just want to find out what really happened to Harvey and where his remains are. I want to know whether he was properly cremated."

She also wants people to know: "You can't trust anyone these days."
