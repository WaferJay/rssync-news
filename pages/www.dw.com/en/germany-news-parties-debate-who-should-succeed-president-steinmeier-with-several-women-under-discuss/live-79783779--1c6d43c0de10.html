Berlin man who drugged and raped women jailed for 12 years

A Berlin court has sentenced a 69-year-old man to 12 years in prison for drugging, raping and filming women he met through online dating platforms.

The defendant had "deliberately sought out victims," presiding judge Jakob Weber said. Judges also ordered preventive detention after his sentence, finding that he remained a danger to the public.

The electrician, who sometimes posed as a police officer or military pilot, was convicted of 22 counts of rape involving 14 women between 2016 and 2022.

At his Berlin apartment, he spiked their drinks with sedatives before attacking them. The women only discovered what had happened during the police investigation.

Investigators found a camera aimed at his bed that ran "24/7," alongside meticulously kept records of recordings, a police officer told the court.

An investigator described "extreme dehumanization" in online groups the man used, saying women were "traded as commodities."

The defendant admitted the rapes at the start of his trial. A psychiatric expert found no evidence that his ability to control his actions was diminished.

Investigations into suspected offenses involving another 44 women are continuing. The ruling can still be appealed.

BASF presses case for Evonik takeover

German chemicals giant BASF has stepped up its push to acquire rival Evonik, arguing that a combination would create a more competitive and resilient global business.

BASF CEO Markus Kamieth said the companies' strengths complemented each other and a deal would make sense as consolidation gathers pace across Europe's chemical industry.

But he stressed that any takeover must benefit BASF shareholders. "We will not pay an excessive price," he said, declining to discuss the status of talks or a timetable.

BASF has raised its offer for Essen-based Evonik to €24 per share, valuing the company at just over €11 billion, the German dpa news agency reported.

The proposal has drawn criticism from Dennis Radtke, who heads the workers' wing of the center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU). He warned that thousands of jobs could be lost and called for the takeover attempt to be abandoned.

Evonik's largest shareholder, the RAG Foundation, holds around 43% of its shares, giving it a key role in any deal.

Girl seriously injured in animal attack at German wildlife park

An eight-year-old girl was seriously injured in an animal attack at a wildlife park south of Berlin, police said.

The girl was inside an enclosure with two other children and a staff member when at least one animal attacked her. The others suffered no visible injuries.

Mercedes cuts profit outlook as China sales slump

German carmaker Mercedes-Benz has lowered its annual profit forecast as falling sales in China and rising costs overshadow a sharp increase in quarterly earnings.

The Stuttgart-based company now expects earnings before interest and taxes to remain at last year's level, having previously forecast a substantial increase.

Market conditions had become more challenging, particularly in China, CEO Ola Källenius said. Mercedes also expects higher costs in the fourth quarter, including for raw materials.

Third-quarter net profit rose 28% to €1.52 billion, although the comparison reflects restructuring costs that weighed on earnings a year earlier. Revenue fell 4.5% to €30.7 billion.

Car sales in China plunged 31%, pushing overall sales down 8% to 407,200 vehicles. Outside China, sales rose 1%.

Fully electric car sales provided a brighter spot, climbing 61%. Källenius also pointed to continued sales growth in Europe and the US.

Merz: Trade allies could 'own the restaurant' together

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz has proposed a global alliance to defend international trade rules and coordinate responses to economic pressure.

Speaking in Tokyo, Merz called for like-minded countries to strengthen and reform the World Trade Organization (WTO), according to his speech manuscript.

Members could commit to rejecting unlawful tariffs and economic coercion, he said, in an apparent reference to the US and China.

They could also agree to accept trade dispute rulings as binding despite the current paralysis of the WTO's appeals body, and respond jointly when other countries break the rules.

Merz urged Germany, Japan and other partners to work more closely together, echoing Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney's call for an alliance of middle powers.

Acting together would mean countries no longer had to ask whether they were "on the menu" or "at the table," Merz said: "Because then we would jointly own the restaurant."

Who is in the running to succeed Steinmeier?

We've already mentioned some potential successors to German President Frank-Walter Steinmeier in this blog. Here's a broader look at the names being discussed.

Much of the debate has focused on choosing a woman, as Germany has never had a female head of state. Potential candidates include:

  • Ilse Aigner: Bavaria's state parliament president, from the conservative Christian Social Union (CSU). She has backing from CSU leader Markus Söder and senior Social Democrats and Greens.
  • Julia Klöckner: Bundestag president, from Chancellor Friedrich Merz's center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU).
  • Karin Prien: Federal education and family minister, also from the CDU.
  • Annegret Kramp-Karrenbauer: Former CDU leader and defense minister.
  • Ines Claus: CDU parliamentary group leader in the state of Hesse.
  • Juli Zeh: Author and constitutional court judge in Brandenburg, and a member of the center-left Social Democratic Party (SPD).
  • Ursula von der Leyen: European Commission president and CDU member.

However, a woman is not certain to get the job. The state premier of Hesse, Boris Rhein, has also been mentioned, while comedian and author Hape Kerkeling is the subject of a public petition rather than a party nomination.

Ines Claus: a possible Steinmeier successor

Nina Haase in Tokyo

Could the name of the next German president be Ines Claus? The discussions around Frank-Walter Steinmeier’s successor, whose term ends next year, have reached the German Chancellor Friedrich Merz on his trip across the Indo Pacific.

Steinmeier will have spent two terms in office when he retires next year. And while the president’s role is largely a ceremonial one in Germany, the process of picking a suitable candidate is a highly political affair, happening largely behind closed doors, unlike in many other countries.

That process is now in full swing. There’s largely bipartisan agreement that the next president should be a woman, which would be a first for the country. Chancellor Merz was one of those early on saying he was in favour of a woman taking the job, but had otherwise stayed out of the discussion so far.

That has now changed. The strong positioning by the Bavarian conservatives from the CSU in favor of Ilse Aigner, a former state agriculture minister, taking the job,  seems to have pushed its bigger sister party, the CDU, to find a candidate from their own ranks.

Ines Claus, a lawyer and legal expert, was named by some within the CDU during Merz's Asia trip. It’s unusual for the chancellor to comment on party political discussions while travelling, but his team has now stressed that they are awaiting "an orderly process." In other words, choosing Steinmeier's successor is far from a done deal.

The CDU is reportedly preparing to discuss who they should put forward at their next board meeting on November 9. They will also take into account the wishes of the CSU, the Social Democrats (SPD), and even the Greens, wanting the future head of state to have broad public support.

German parties weigh candidates to succeed Steinmeier

German political parties are weighing who should succeed President Frank-Walter Steinmeier, with Bavarian parliamentary speaker Ilse Aigner attracting support across party lines. 

The presidency is Germany's largely ceremonial head-of-state role, separate from the chancellor, who leads the government. Steinmeier cannot seek a third consecutive term, and his successor will be elected on January 30.

Germany has never before had a woman as federal president.

Aigner belongs to the conservative Christian Social Union (CSU), the Bavarian sister party of Merz's center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU). The CSU leader and Bavarian premier Markus Söder has endorsed her.

She has also drawn backing from politicians outside the conservative bloc, including senior Greens politician and Bundestag deputy speaker Omid Nouripour.

"We need someone in these times who holds the country together," Nouripour said during Steinmeier's state visit to China, arguing that Aigner had the experience and authority required.

Center-left Social Democratic Party politicians have also voiced support.

But Merz's CDU says it should nominate the candidate. Sources close to the chancellor denied reports that he favored Ines Claus, who leads the CDU parliamentary group in the state of Hesse, saying he had "neither made a decision nor put forward a proposal."

10/28/2026October 28, 2026

Most Germans plan to sit out Halloween, poll finds

Nearly three-quarters of Germans are unlikely to celebrate Halloween, despite its popularity among younger adults, according to a YouGov poll.

Some 74% said they were very or fairly unlikely to take part, whether by dressing up, attending a party, decorating or handing out sweets to children.

By contrast, 23% said they would probably celebrate in some way. That figure rose to 50% among 18- to 24-year-olds, compared with just 11% among those aged 55 and over.

The survey found no difference between respondents in eastern and western Germany.

YouGov surveyed 2,666 adults in Germany on October 27. The poll was described as representative.

Halloween falls on Saturday this year. Its name comes from "All Hallows' Eve," the evening before the Christian feast of All Saints, and its customs spread to the US with European immigrants.
