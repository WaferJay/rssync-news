German business bets on Ukraine despite war damage

Sabine Kinkartz
11/24/2026November 24, 2026

German companies remain committed to Ukraine despite million-dollar losses, an unreliable energy supply and a shortage of skilled workers. They are betting on new markets and long-term growth.

Power plants, train stations, shopping centers, warehouses and residential buildings: The massive Russian attacks on civilian targets across Ukraine continue unabated. Industrial facilities have also come under fire, affecting German companies.

"Over the past few months, goods and facilities worth several hundred million euros have gone up in smoke," reported Oliver Gierlichs, who heads Bayer's operations in Ukraine and serves as president of the German-Ukrainian Chamber of Industry and Commerce (AHK Ukraine). The damage is estimated at €500 million ($544 million).

At the 9th German-Ukrainian Business Forum, held in Berlin on November 20, Gierlichs described the situation on the ground. Together with two other German business associations, the AHK had invited around 500 participants from politics and industry, including German Chancellor Friedrich Merz and Ukrainian Prime Minister Serhii Koretskyi.

According to the AHK, just over 2,000 German companies are active in Ukraine. German direct investment in the country totals around €1 billion ($1.1 billion). The situation remains critical due to the ongoing attacks, Gierlichs said. "We are preparing for a difficult winter."

Made in Germany — Rebuilding Ukraine

Ukrainian economy under pressure

After posting comparatively strong growth rates in recent years, Ukraine's economy has slowed sharply since the summer.

"The intensified Russian attacks on Ukraine's critical infrastructure and production capacity in recent months have significantly worsened the economic outlook," according to the latest analysis by the Kyiv School of Economics (KSE), which warns of "substantial risks."

Energy supplies are under strain, and electricity remains in short supply. Companies are also grappling with a worker shortage, as many people have been mobilized for military service or have fled abroad to escape the attacks. German business leaders also cite burdensome approval processes and unclear regulations in Ukraine.

"Not a single German company has left Ukraine since the start of this war," Merz emphasized at the business forum. He also pointed out that "in 2025, trade between Germany and Ukraine exceeded €12 billion, an increase of 4.4% compared with the previous year."

Benefits extend beyond Ukraine

Military cooperation currently tops the agenda in German-Ukrainian relations. Germany continues to supply large quantities of defense equipment, but new capabilities are increasingly being developed through joint ventures. At the business forum, held at the House of German Business Associations in Berlin, the long-range combat drone "Anubis," produced by the German-Ukrainian joint venture Auterion Airlogix, was displayed in the foyer. The drone was developed in Ukraine and the software in Germany.

A total of 5,000 drones are to be produced near Munich. This marks the first time Germany has established industrial-scale production of heavy attack drones on its own soil. "Through this cooperation, Germany's technological capacity for innovation is expanding in ways that would have been unimaginable just a few years ago," Merz said.

The chancellor is convinced that cooperation between the two countries will also benefit Germany's civilian industries. "What we invest in Ukraine today will be repaid many times over through stability, export opportunities, joint projects, and economic growth," he said.

Half a trillion dollars needed for reconstruction

For foreign companies operating in Ukraine, it is a continuous balancing act. On the one hand, substantial risks persist; on the other, Ukraine represents a land of opportunity, with a market size virtually unmatched anywhere else in Europe. In February 2025, the European Commission and the Ukrainian government estimated that Ukraine would need $524 billion in funding for reconstruction and recovery over the following 10 years. This figure has likely grown even larger since then.

In this context, Ukraine is increasingly seen not merely as a recipient of aid but also as a potential future business destination. The country aims to modernize its economy, bring it into alignment with EU standards, and achieve deeper integration into the European single market over the long term.

Can Ukraine still count on Germany?

Mechanical engineering, energy and infrastructure opportunities 

"We see significant opportunities in the defense industry, the energy sector, infrastructure, information technology and many other industries," Ukrainian Prime Minister Koretskyi said in Berlin.

"German expertise and Ukrainian drive, combined with unity and determination, make for a powerful partnership," Oliver Gierlichs noted. However, achieving this will require greater support, the AHK president emphasized. "Companies need reliable financing and investment guarantees, as well as effective instruments to protect themselves against war-related risks."

Reliable logistics, predictable business conditions, and faster administrative procedures will also be essential, he added.

Investment guarantees and reforms as prerequisites for new projects

At the business forum, Chancellor Merz pledged support in these areas while also calling on Ukraine to pursue further reforms, particularly to strengthen the rule of law and combat corruption. "These reforms are a prerequisite for additional financial assistance and investment."

Another challenge stems from the differing interests of the partners. German companies are seeking to expand into new markets, while their Ukrainian counterparts seek technology transfers and local production to keep as much value creation as possible in the country. "Export fees or legal restrictions on the transfer of intellectual property rights stand in the way of joint innovation efforts," the chancellor cautioned.

High-tech revolution in defense

Who is financing Ukraine's reconstruction?

Representatives of Ukrainian business associations estimate that the private sector could cover up to 40% of Ukraine's reconstruction costs in the long term. For now, however, many projects rely on support from international development banks, government guarantees, or public funding programs. Without such support, many investments would be difficult to undertake.

In principle, business investment follows a straightforward model: Companies commit their own capital and bear the losses if a venture fails commercially. When bank loans are involved, the risk is initially shared by the company and the lending institution. Government-backed projects are different. When investment guarantees are in place, companies are compensated for any losses incurred.

This does not apply when companies incur losses as a result of their own business decisions. Compensation may, however, be available in cases involving political actions, such as expropriation, or when war destroys assets and disrupts operations. Under a government guarantee, the taxpayer ultimately bears the risk in such circumstances. By the end of 2025, the German government had provided investment guarantees covering a total investment volume of €558 million ($611 million) in Ukraine.

German companies hoping to benefit from Ukraine's reconstruction need to establish partnerships and build a long-term presence in the country now. After all, the competition is already heating up. At the German-Ukrainian Business Forum, participants noted that German business leaders have often been more risk-averse than their counterparts from Turkey, India and China. International interest in Ukraine's reconstruction is considerable, and German companies are far from the only ones vying for contracts.
