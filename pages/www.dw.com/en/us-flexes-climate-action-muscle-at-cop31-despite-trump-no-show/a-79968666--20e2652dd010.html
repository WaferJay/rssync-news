US flexes climate action muscle at COP31 despite Trump no-show

Natalie Muller | Tim Schauenberg in Antalya
11/17/2026November 17, 2026

The US government is snubbing UN climate talks in Turkey. That's prompted a delegation of American lawmakers, mayors and governors to make the trip instead, aiming to show climate action in the US is alive and well.

The United States, isn't at the table at the UN climate conference in the Turkish coastal city of Antalya. It's the second consecutive year that President Donald Trump, who has repeatedly said he believes climate change is a hoax, has not sent a federal delegation to the annual summit.

But that doesn't mean the US is totally absent. Far from it, since more than 120 state and local leaders from across the country are attending as part of the "America Is All In" coalition.

"We understand the importance of showing up. And if others refuse to do so, we will," said Chris Wheat, executive director of the coalition. 

It's crucial, he added, to show the world that "millions of Americans are continuing to press forward on climate action" — even if the message from Washington, D.C., is a different one. 

Historically, the US has released more emissions than any other country, and it is currently the world's second-biggest emitter of planet-warming greenhouse gases after China. Since taking office in January 2025, Trump has actively promoted the use of oil, coal and gas, the fossil fuels that are heating the planet and contributing to an increase in extreme weather events. 

The president has also cut funds for clean energy projects and rolled back some of the climate policies of his predecessors, such as rules limiting power plant emissions.

Momentum beyond White House

The unofficial US delegation of mainly Democrat lawmakers, mayors, governors and ex-officials — including former Secretary of State John Kerry, former Vice President Al Gore and California Governor Gavin Newsom — are not taking part in the climate negotiations. But they have been holding discussions at events on the conference sidelines.

They are keen to send a message to the international community that, despite their government's absence, efforts to transition to clean energy are gathering pace at the state level.

Does Gen Z care about the UN's big climate summit?

Mike Williams, a senior fellow at the Center for American Progress, an independent liberal public policy research institute based in Washington D.C., said the presence of US lawmakers in Antalya sends an important signal that "there's quite a lot of us in the US that are still deeply invested in this. It's not just the person sitting in the White House."

Nearly half of US states, representing about 60% of the US economy, are committed to reducing emissions in line with the Paris Agreement — the 2015 global pact to limit global warming. Many have implemented initiatives such as tax incentives for renewables and other policies designed to curb emissions.

A report by America Is All In and the University of Maryland's Center for Global Sustainability found that current state policies, combined with market trends and falling technology costs, could slash US carbon emissions by up to 46% below 2005 levels by 2035.

States tout climate action

"Even if we find a hostile federal government, they're not going to be able to stop the momentum," Minnesota Governor and Kamala Harris' running mate during the 2024 presidential election Tim Walz told a crowd at the summit in Turkey, adding that his state was on track to have a "100% carbon free" grid by 2037, earlier than their target of 2040.

Walz said the state was also producing sustainable aviation fuel to reduce flight emissions, manufacturing green steel and increasing the share of electric vehicles. "It's all moving forward," he said. "So this false choice that we need to choose the economy over clean energy — that has been totally rejected."

New Mexico Governor Michelle Lujan Grisham said her state — the second-largest oil and gas producer in the US — had significantly cut emissions from the potent greenhouse gas methane through stricter regulations. She also touted measures such as shutting down coal power plants and expanding wind and solar energy.

"The largest wind farm in North America is in New Mexico," she said. "We're going to show the federal government, I am undaunted by their lack of responsibility and effort in cleaning up fossil fuels."

What's next for US climate policy after midterms?

The climate conference comes shortly after the US midterm elections, in which Trump's Republicans suffered a significant defeat. Democrats in Antalya have been keen to reassure the international community that the US would return to the COP table in the event of a change of government in two years.

Jorge Gastelumendi, senior director of the Atlantic Council's Climate Resilience Center, noted that US credibility around climate change and foreign policy in general had been "badly damaged" under Trump. "And so it's going to be a matter of how that could be built back up."

Meanwhile, Governor Newsom of California, a state of around 40 million people that plans to get 100% of its energy from renewables by 2045, said the US administration's climate withdrawal was also hurting the country's competitiveness.

COP31: Can electrification replace fossil fuels?

"We need to reengage in these international agreements, and we need to recognize that China is outpacing us in many critical areas," such as solar power and electric cars, he told reporters in Antalya. "There is no substitute for national leadership."

Williams from the Center for American Progress said he wouldn't hold it against any countries wary about the US returning to the climate negotiations after two exits under Trump.

"Nobody should be under any perception that the US would be welcomed back with open arms," he said. "I think there needs to be an effort to show a willingness to collaborate […] That's another reason why it's important we're here now to show that there is this very large undercurrent of support within the US in spite of what's happening."
