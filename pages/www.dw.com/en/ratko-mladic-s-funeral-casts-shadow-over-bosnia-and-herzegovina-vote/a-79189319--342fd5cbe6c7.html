Bosnia: The long shadow cast by Ratko Mladic's coffin

Dragan Maksimovic in Banja Luka, Bosnia and Herzegovina
09/08/2026September 8, 2026

Ratko Mladic’s funeral in Belgrade, attended by Serbian and Bosnian Serb leaders, has deepened regional tensions and could affect the upcoming election in Bosnia and Herzegovina.

Belgrade has made a point of insisting that it did not organize a state funeral for former Bosnian Serb army commander Ratko Mladic, who was convicted of genocide in Srebrenica and crimes against humanity in 2017.

But when the body of a war criminal is flown home accompanied by the country's justice minister, when ceremonies unfold with military honors in state-controlled venues in the presence of senior political leaders and when clear condemnation of glorification of the deceased is absent, the message is unambiguous.

The impact of this message is being felt beyond Serbia's borders.

In Bosnia and Herzegovina — the state created by the 1995 Dayton Peace Agreement, which comprises two entities linked by a weak central government — reactions were as deeply divided as the country itself.

Bosnia and Herzegovina: Two entities, two narratives

Republika Srpska, the Serb-majority entity covering about 49% of the country's territory, sent its officials to the funeral.

Public discourse there was dominated by narratives of "defending the people" and "the injustice of international courts."

"Without Ratko Mladic, there would be no Republika Srpska," was the refrain among thousands of mourners at the funeral in Belgrade.

Sarajevo, the seat of the central government of Bosnia and Herzegovina, sees this approach as a continued policy of relativizing war crimes and a threat to stability.

Bosnia and Herzegovina cools diplomatic ties with Serbia

In the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina, the entity where Bosniaks and Croats form the majority, the response was very different and the tone sharp.

Foreign Minister Elmedin Konakovic described Serbia's official response as a conscious choice by Belgrade, not something imposed on it.

He announced measures to cool diplomatic ties with Serbia, withdrawing almost all staff from the country's embassy in Belgrade, leaving only the ambassador, explaining that he lacks the authority to close the embassy but can reduce its presence to a minimum. The move effectively froze day-to-day diplomatic communication on the ground.

"We will pull out everyone we can; consular services will be relocated to Zagreb and Podgorica," he said.

Denis Becirovic, chair of the Presidency of Bosnia and Herzegovina, called the funeral with military honors an "anti-civilizational act" and warned of far-reaching consequences for the entire region.

For representatives of Mladic's victims, any public glorification of the former general is a direct insult to the families of those killed.

Political point-scoring

Mladic's death and funeral are not just about the past.

At a time when both Serbia and Bosnia and Herzegovina are facing general elections — Bosnia on October 4 and Serbia likely just a few weeks later — emotions become a resource.

In both countries, parts of the electorate still see Mladic as a symbol of resistance, which means that every gesture in support of him scores political points with these voters.

As the general election in Bosnia and Herzegovina approaches, sharp condemnations from Sarajevo are seen as evidence that the central government is "defending the state" and "the dignity of victims." This, too, is being turned into political capital.

In Republika Srpska, the presence of officials at the funeral and the narrative of "injustice" strengthen the position of those building their identity in opposition to international rulings.

Yet there are no "winners." The more political points anyone scores on this issue, the worse relations become and the further accession to the EU recedes into the distance — for both countries.

Tanja Topic, a political analyst from Banja Luka, is clear about who is responsible for these political narratives: "The main culprits are the political elites, because in 30 years we have not moved forward. There has been no critical confrontation with the dark side of history," she says. "This is a darkening of civilizational consciousness."

Topic went on to say that Mladic's funeral "is a welcome gift to the protagonists of ethno-nationalist policies" and noted that these people still hold power in Bosnia and Herzegovina.

"Such an institutional stance towards crimes provokes a reaction on the other side, and then everyone exploits the situation, trapping us in a vicious circle where each tries to extract political profit," Topic says.

A divided society and frozen relations

After Mladic's funeral, the Office of the High Representative (OHR) in Bosnia, which was created after the war to oversee the implementation of the US-brokered Dayton Peace Agreement, stressed that Bosnia's future cannot be built on glorifying those responsible for the worst crimes of the past.

"Glorifying convicted war criminals, as well as denying genocide and other war crimes, creates divisions, inflicts additional pain on victims and their families, and undermines the reconciliation process," said the OHR.

Bosnia remains a deeply divided society that has not moved forward in the 31 years since the war ended.

The fact that the ideology that helped spark that war still lives on, means that Mladic's funeral did not close a chapter in the region's history, it tore open a new one, measuring the Balkans' willingness to step out of the shadow of the 1990s.

While politicians seek to score political points, citizens pay the price of frozen relations.

Edited by: Aingeal Flanagan

Dragan Maksimovic Reporter, writer and correspondent based in Banja Luka, Bosnia and Herzegovina@knespoljac
