'Coalition of the Willing' meets in Berlin over Ukraine's future

Jens Thurau
11/18/2026November 18, 2026

The "Coalition of the Willing" supporting Ukraine is gathering in Berlin. One of the key issues under discussion is whether Europe will be able to play a role in future negotiations over the country's future.

Once again, Berlin is rolling out the red carpet, and once again the focus is on Ukraine: Around 20 heads of state and government, primarily from across Europe, are set to gather in the German capital on Thursday (November 19). They will discuss the future of the Eastern European country that has been under attack from Russia since February 2022 and is now facing yet another harsh winter.

Extensive security measures have already been announced for Berlin's government district, pointing to the presence of high-ranking participants. It remains unclear whether Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy will be among them. What does appear certain, however, is the attendance of chief US negotiators Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, according to several media outlets.

The "Coalition of the Willing" is a group of 36 countries determined not to be deterred from supporting the nation under attack. Unlike other international forums, the group is focused not only on how to support Ukraine militarily and financially in the here and now.

DW interview with German Chancellor Friedrich Merz

Led by Germany, France and the United Kingdom, the coalition is also prepared, in principle, to help monitor a possible ceasefire within Ukraine itself and to contribute to the complex security guarantees the country would then require, should such a scenario come to pass. The effort dates back to March 2025, when the countries involved first met in London.

But what can the coalition realistically achieve? Stefan Meister of the German Council on Foreign Relations (DGAP) is not overly optimistic.

"The fundamental problem is that Europe has yet to put forward a proposal," he told DW. "There is no plan outlining what security guarantees Europe would offer, what troops it would be prepared to deploy, what measures it could provide to safeguard a ceasefire, or where it would be willing to make compromises."

Merz: 'Germany has a seat at the table'

Nevertheless, the meeting comes at a moment when some observers believe there may finally be movement toward ending the war. During talks in Abu Dhabi in the United Arab Emirates a few days ago, Russia, Ukraine and the US managed to agree only on a prisoner exchange. It was a modest step forward.

Further talks are planned, and it has been a long time since the prospects appeared as favorable as they do now for Europe to secure a role in future decisions on war and peace in Ukraine.

Western allies boost Ukraine's defenses but clash over deep strikes

It has repeatedly been argued, both by US officials and by those in power in Russia, that Europe does not speak with a single voice.

This has sparked a debate among EU heads of state and government over who, exactly, could represent Europe in such talks. Could it be German Chancellor Friedrich Merz of the center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU)?

After the EU summit in Dublin, Ireland, on Friday, Merz said: "For the first time, concrete peace talks are expected to take place in the coming weeks with Europe represented. Germany has a seat at the table, that is absolutely clear."

Europe has yet to speak with one voice

But what exactly did that mean? That Germany alone would speak for all of Europe? Hardly. As was also made clear in Berlin on Monday, Europe would be represented by the E3 countries, namely Germany, France, and the non-EU member United Kingdom, joined by Poland and Italy. Official EU representatives, including European Council President Antonio Costa, would also take part.

Stefan Meister of the DGAP believes the debate says a great deal about the pressures Europe itself is under. 

"Europe's positioning has more to do with domestic political considerations than with developments in the negotiations," Meister said. "Everyone needs to show that they are participating in the talks in some way. Elections will be held next year in France and Poland, and it is still unclear whether French support for Ukraine will continue in its current form after that."

German Deputy Government Spokesperson Sebastian Hille, meanwhile, sought to downplay the debate on Monday over a possible role for Friedrich Merz as chief negotiator.

At the federal government's regular press briefing, he said: "This is not about discussing individuals. What matters is that Putin finally understands that he must come to the negotiating table so that we have a chance of achieving peace in the first place."

Can Germany make Europe pay more for Ukraine?

German government spokesperson: Europe now needs stamina

US negotiator Jared Kushner, the son-in-law of President Donald Trump, struck a rather different tone last week.

Speaking in Abu Dhabi, he said: "If Europe could identify one person to speak on their behalf, that would be a bigger achievement than getting the Russians to include them in the negotiations."

Government spokesperson Hille responded on Monday: "Europe's strength lies precisely in the unity and cohesion of many countries. We should not allow ourselves to be drawn into debates that others want to frame around this question."

Hille emphasized that the situation remained unchanged: Peace in Ukraine could only be achieved step by step. Europe, too, would need stamina, perseverance and a willingness to undertake the painstaking work required to bring it about.

Declining support for Ukraine in German polls

European supporters of Ukraine are also under pressure as public opinion on the issue is shifting, including in Germany. While a broad majority favored helping defend the country in the immediate aftermath of Russia's invasion in the spring of 2022, support has since fallen markedly.

According to the latest DeutschlandTrend survey on the issue, conducted or German public broadcaster ARD in September 2026, 52% of eligible voters still support weapons deliveries to Ukraine, while 42% believe such support has gone too far. That is the highest level of opposition recorded since the start of the war.

A further study conducted for public broadcaster WDR in November 2026 found that support for Ukraine has become a more polarizing issue in Germany than immigration. According to the survey, 54% of respondents favor continued support for Ukraine, while just under 29% would prefer a rapprochement with Russia, despite its war of aggression against Ukraine.
