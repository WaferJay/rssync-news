What you need to know

  • Merz said he was 'grateful' his governing alliance was backing him but faces pressure to soften his reform agenda.
  • Merz's political future was in sharp focus after his governing CDU party was ousted from the state parliament in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania for the first time in Germany's postwar history.
  • The far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) was the strongest party in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, narrowly ahead of the center-left Social Democratic Party, or the junior coalition partner in Merz's federal coalition government.
  • The socialist Left Party finished first in Berlin, also ahead of Merz's CDU.
  • AfD co-chairs celebrated the election result, saying the 'blue wave cannot be stopped.'

CDU's leader in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania resigns

The Christian Democrat's lead candidate in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania has quit after the party slumped below the 5% hurdle and lost its place in the state parliament. 

Daniel Peters said in a statement late on Monday that he was drawing the personal consequences "from the resounding defeat" in the state. 

His announcement followed a meeting of the CDU leadership at the local level in the state capital Schwerin. 

Peters, 45, said that on a temporary basis Philipp Amthor, currently a junior minister for cooperation between the federal and state governments, would be taking over the CDU leadership in the state. 

Two other members of the four-person party steering committee, Katy Hoffmeister and Ann Christin von Allwörden, had already announced their resignations after the CDU's worst showing in any state election since 1949. 

Philipp Amthor, 33, was deputy party chair in the state and the last remaining member of the quartet. On a national level, he's probably best known as a former leader of the CDU youth wing.

He became something of a boy wonder in his 20s, rising the ranks in national politics until 2020. Then, a lobbying scandal laid him low, forced his resignation, and set his career trajectory back several years.

AfD does not expect to govern Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania

The AfD's lead candidate in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, Leif Erik Holm, has said he no longer expects his party to be part of the state government, despite emerging as the largest single party on Sunday. 

Speaking on ZDF public television on Monday evening, Holm said his party was no longer sending out invitations for coalition or government involvement negotiations to other parties.

He said the other groups had already declined and said they wanted to try to form a three-way alliance of the Social Democrats, the Left Party and the Greens. 

"We are now preparing ourselves for the role of opposition, and we will be a strong opposition," Holm said. 

Until now the state was governed by a two-party coalition, the SPD and the Left. Now the only combination not including the AfD that can command a majority will need to involve all three of the left-leaning parties. Between them, they will have 39 seats against the AfD's 32.

Merkel evokes Adenauer — says CDU should try to 'build bridges'

Former Chancellor Angela Merkel, probably the best known Christian Democrat from the east of Germany where the CDU is now suffering such serious setbacks, has said the party needs to remember its roots as a unifier and a party of the masses at this difficult time. 

She said lessons could be taken from Germany's first post-war chancellor, Konrad Adenauer of the CDU, that still applied today. 

"For him it was clear that you had to build bridges," Merkel said. "And at the same time it automatically became clear who you could not build any bridges with." 

Merkel — whose constituency when she was a lawmaker was in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania on the Baltic coast — was speaking at an exhibit opening in the Bundestag parliament featuring two famous portraits of former chancellors, one of them depicting Adenauer. 

"On a day like today and after an evening like yesterday it's perhaps worth contemplating again, what can we take from Konrad Adenauer?" Merkel asked. 

She said that history had taught him that it was important to be inclusive and to speak to everyone but also to be "aware of your own values."

Merkel said that the former mayor of Cologne, imprisoned multiple times during the Nazi era, saw the CDU as made up of "three equal roots — conservative, liberal and christian-social." 

She said he saw the need for bridges between workers and employers, men and women, environment and the economy, the city and the countryside, between religions and generations. "And I would add, between people with and without a migrant background," Merkel said. 

"I think that needs to be the aspiration of a popular party  that's what he bequeathed us," Merkel said of Adenauer. "And this bridge-building, which certainly has not got any easier these days, must continue."

SPD seeks to put cost of living 'at the heart of our politics' amid decline

The Social Democrats (SPD) have issued a hasty written resolution in the aftermath of September's elections saying the party wants to pivot its focus to inflation and cost of living issues. 

"For years prices have been rising in our country," the resolution issued by the party steering committee on Monday said. "Therefore we are moving the question of the cost of living into the heart of out politics." 

The paper said this would include a cap on fuel prices and moves to ease care costs. The document also said that the party, junior coalition partner to Merz's CDU at the federal level, stood by its role in government. 

"But after these state elections, we can't simply carry on as before," the paper says. The party leadership said that Germany needed changes but that these needed to be just and to constitute real improvements for citizens. 

"Above all employees, people on small and medium incomes, and families cannot be the ones who bear the brunt of the burden of the reforms," the paper says. 

Among other changes, the party calls for reforms to inheritance tax and rules on gifts in a bid to finance "a real education offensive at the state level." 

"Our work in govermment is important, but a comeback of social democracy will require more," the party leaders write. 

The SPD also led Germany's previous three-party federal coalition govermemnt.

Berlin now lets 16- and 17-year-old's vote, but not all election helpers realized this

For the first time on Sunday, Berlin allowed 16- and 17-year-olds to vote in state elections, in a country where the nationwide voting age is 18. 

But there was one slight snag: not all the helpers at polling booths had heard the news. 

The head of Berlin's state election office, David Czerny, said he received reports of around 20 isolated cases, in six different districts, where young Berliners were initially and incorrectly told they were not eligible to vote and were sent away.

Czerny said that despite the topic having been raised in training sessions for volunteers, some were nevertheless unaware of the change. 

He said that in the vast majority of the cases, it was possible to correct the error. When contact details were avaiable, authorities reached out to the young would-be voters directly. 

"In those cases where we had no contact information, we sent out a member of the election commission on site to knock on doors," Czerny said. 

Another lead election official Stephan Bröchler described the shortcomings as "very irritating." He stressed that all staff involved had or should have been briefed on the change. 

"We will certainly check to see if we can make this aspect even more prominent in our training program," he said.

Berlin's Eralp says she still opposes Olympic bid and expects it to fail

Berlin's likely next mayor Elif Eralp says Sunday's vote has not changed her stance on the city's bid to host the Oympics in the coming years. 

Berlin elected not to hold a referendum on an Olympic bid — as Munich and the Rhine/Ruhr region led by Cologne did — instead putting the motion through the city's parliament. The Left was one of the parties to oppose the motion, which passed with a narrow majority. 

"I think that the decision has resolved itself, because we also won this election in part with our position on the Olympics," Eralp said, adding that this meant those voting on the move "will also have to consider whether it makes sense to award the bid to such a candidate." 

This coming Saturday, Germany's DOSB Olympic federation is set to decide on which German city will bid for an upcoming Summer Games, and whether they will target 2036, 2040, or 2044. 

Berlin was already considered an outside shot as candidate, not least given the less public approach to securing public support, asking legislators rather than citizens directly.

Given that Hamburg had to abort an advanced Olympic bid in 2015, when residents voted it down at referendum, Berlin's reticence to gain a clear grassroots endorsement was already seen as a drawback to its candidacy.

Eralp tried to make clear to the DOSB voting panel that they should consider looking elsewhere, not to the hosts of the 1936 Summer Games. 

"I have already quite clearly said, for instance, that I consider the €6 million (almost $7 million) already spent on the Olympic bid to be a waste of taxpayer's money," Eralp said. "And many millions and billions are supposed to follow. I see no gain here for Berlin."

Left defends Neukölln after-party, as organized crime boss, Gaza activist attend

Images from the Left's election party in Berlin's Neukölln district as the results came in have prompted headlines and critical attention. 

A notorious figure from Berlin's organized crime underworld, Issa Remmo, was pictured at the socialist party's celebrations in the southern district, forcing the party to say he was neither invited nor registered on the guest list. 

"We were not aware of this person or their attendance in particular, they were not on our registration list," the Left's Neukölln party offices said in response on Monday. 

Rival parties like the CDU and the Greens responded critically, with Berlin's now-outgoing mayor Kai Wegner (CDU) saying that Remmo's appearance sent a "disturbing signal" from a party "that wants to take over responsibility for our city." 

The German DPA news agency reported that one of its correspondents, sent to the party after the news of Remmo's attendance had broken to follow up, was not permitted entry. 

The Left also faced criticism for the attendance of a pro-Palestinian activist who is allegedly linked to the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine (PFLP). This is the second-largest faction of the Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO) after the more moderate Fatah, but unlike Fatah, it is deemed a terrorist organization by the EU and US.

Ferat Kocak, a Left Party lawmaker for the district, was pictured with the activist. He said in response that he distanced himself from any and all appeals to violence.

But Kocak said he took "hundreds of photos" with admirers and supporters at the party, which was fundamentally open to anybody. 

"When people come up to me at our election parties and want to take a photo, I assume at first that it's ok," Kocak said. 

Alleged antisemitism had been an issue for the Left on the campaign trail, after a Berlin rapper sang contentious slogans including "Death to the IDF [Israel Defense Force]" at a rally last month.

Merz wants to discuss 'unfair' German health insurance

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz acknowledged on Monday that the different medical treatment on offer to people with standard or private health insurance in Germany is seen as "unfair" by some parts of the population.

By law, people in Germany are required to have standard health insurance with monthly contributions matched by their employers. If they can afford it, people are also free to pay for their own private health insurance – which can often cut waiting times for medical appointments and treatment dramatically.

Merz said the "parallel existence" of both systems can lead to a "sense of injustice" among the population.

"We see the problem," he said. "When people see this as such a big problem, and I share the assessment, then we have to change something. And that's exactly what we'll be talking about."

By taking into consideration the disadvantages felt by people who can't afford private health insurance, Merz (CDU) may be able to persuade his center-left Social Democrat (SPD) coalition partners to support other reforms in areas such as pensions.

Bavarian State Premier Markus Söder (CSU) called on the center-left Social Democrats to decide "quickly" who is "in charge" after a heated election campaign in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania in which the SPD was highly critical of the coalition government of which it is also part.

Bavaria's Söder warns of 'blue wave and red front'

Markus Söder, the Bavarian state premier and the leader of the Christian Social Union (CSU), the Bavarian sister party of Chancellor Friedrich Merz's Christian Democratic Union (CDU), has described the weekend's elections in Berlin and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania as a "total write-off" for the conservative "Union."

"The extremes finished in front," he said on Monday. "AfD and Left Party. Blue wave and red front."

Söder said the CDU's failure to reach the minimum 5% threshold to even enter the state parliament in northeastern Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania was an "historic event in a negative sense."

The conservative heavyweight also warned against the "left-wing coalitions" which are likely to be formed in the two states, especially in Berlin, where he accused the socialist Left Party of sharing the same unconstitutional goals as the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) and of being "deeply antisemitic."

The Left Party has frequently been dogged by accusations of antisemitism in connection with its criticism of Israel and support of Palestinians both in the Middle East and in Berlin – accusations it denies.

"There is a clear stop line," said the party's parliamentary leader Heidi Reichinnek on Monday. "Anyone who crosses it has no place in this party."

Returning to the election results, Söder advised against a "business as usual" approach from the CDU/CSU, saying: "We have to accept, analyze them and ask: What are citizens telling us? What are the concerns? I believe the majority of people want reforms. So the idea of stripping them back is fundamentally false. Germany needs reforms."

But he did suggest it would be wrong to simply force unpopular reforms through and called for "social balance."

Merz: CDU facing 'fundamental, long-term questions'

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said on Monday that his conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU) must ask itself "fundamental and long-term questions" following its disastrous showing in state elections this month.

Merz said there were "too many questions to which the CDU has no satisfactory answers," adding that the party had to "talk about the work and structure of the party as well as about our program and the profile of the CDU."

The under-fire chancellor said he was determined to restore the CDU to what it "once was and unfortunately only still is in some parts of the country — a big-tent party."

He said he himself would come up with some "very concrete proposals" for change.

Commitment to coalition with SPD

Merz also stressed that he wanted to uphold the ruling coalition with the Social Democrats (SPD), saying that the democratic future of Germany and its integral position in the European Union were at stake.

The SPD also reaffirmed its commitment to the alliance on Monday morning, despite the CDU's poor performance in the recent state elections.

Merz also said that his own position in the CDU had not been called into question.

"Neither yesterday evening nor this morning was there any question regarding personnel, let alone a debate on personnel," he said.

Merz retains CDU backing after party suffers worst-ever election result

Roshni Majumdar Editor

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said on Monday that he retains the backing of his Christian Democratic Union (CDU) despite the conservative party's poor performance in state elections Berlin and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania this weekend.

"Neither yesterday evening nor this morning was there any question regarding personnel, let alone a debate on personnel," he said.

In northeastern Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, the CDU failed to reach the minimum 5% threshold required to enter the state parliament.

"I know that I as an individual played a role in these state elections," Merz said, in a relatively rare admission of personal responsibility. "But the reasons for the results go far beyond just me."

"We are experiencing fundamental change in our political party system, both here in Germany but also abroad," he said, blaming "extremists" who promise "radical, easy solutions."

He lamented that, in Berlin, over 40% of people voted for either the socialist Left Party or the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD), bundling the two together as "destructive forces" which he said "question our entire parliamentary system."

He acknowledged voters' legitimate concerns around jobs, families, pensions and healthcare, and said his governing coalition and the political center had the "historic task" of responding to those concerns – and doing so as part of a European community.

"For eight decades now, we have been part of a western alliance, not only by way of geopgraphy but also through our shared norms," he said.

"Under [postwar chancellor] Konrad Adenauer, we left nationalism behind us. Our historic task now is to ensure that we remain a country anchored in the European Union, facing the storms of our times together with our European partners. This will take a huge effort, an effort I am prepared to make."
