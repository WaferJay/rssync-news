A growing alliance wants to leave fossil fuels behind. Can it deliver?

Natalie Muller
11/16/2026November 16, 2026

In April, about 60 countries held an unprecedented meeting in Colombia to work out a plan to slowly quit oil, coal and gas. How influential is this coalition at the COP31 climate summit?

This time last year, as negotiations at the UN COP climate summit in Brazil entered crunch time, there was hope that governments would agree on a clear roadmap for phasing out oil, gas and coal. More than 80 countries backed the idea.

But instead, after opposition from oil-producing states such as Russia and Saudi Arabia, the conference wrapped up with a watered-down final agreement that did not explicitly mention fossil fuels. 

As a compromise, summit host Brazil offered to produce a "voluntary" roadmap. At the same time, a group of 57 countries frustrated with the deadlock at the consensus-based UN talks decided to form a "coalition of the willing" to accelerate the fossil fuel exit outside the COP framework. 

They held their own summit in April in Santa Marta, Colombia — the first such conference to specifically focus on implementing the transition away from fossil fuels (referred to as TAFF).

What happened in Santa Marta? 

Co-hosted by Colombia and the Netherlands, the Santa Marta conference brought together countries including Canada, Norway, Brazil, Nigeria, Mexico, Australia and Germany, as well as highly climate-vulnerable states such as Vanuatu. 

The idea was for governments to start developing national roadmaps and working out how to restructure their financial and trade systems to wean themselves off polluting energy sources — an incredibly complicated undertaking, particularly for developing countries dependent on revenue from fossil fuel exports.

Part of their discussions focused on cutting subsidies for oil, coal and gas, as well as supporting people whose livelihoods rely on the fossil fuel industry to find new forms of income. 

What role will the 'coalition of the willing' play at COP? 

A little over six months on from the Santa Marta meeting, representatives from almost 200 countries are holding talks in Antalya, Turkey, as part of the latest UN climate conference. 

Countries agreed for the first time in 2023 to transition away from fossil fuels — the main driver behind rising global temperatures. But summits since then have failed to pin down how to implement that pledge. 

It's not yet clear whether the Santa Marta coalition will influence ambition at this year's COP.

"I think it's more of a long-term game," said Chetna Hareesh Kumar, a climate policy analyst at the Berlin-based research organization NewClimate Institute.

She says it's too soon to expect the coalition to have an influence on the negotiations but adds that that could change at future COPs. The grouping could bring more nations on board if, for example, it shows it can be truly inclusive, address the needs of different types of countries and show that a transition is feasible. 

One role the group is playing, according to Alexandra Scott, a senior climate diplomacy adviser at Italian climate change think tank ECCO, is "keeping up the political momentum," and showing "that there is this huge group of countries who really want to accelerate this transition away from fossil fuels."

She says it's also building an invaluable "cadre of knowledge" on how to develop national TAFF plans and deal with barriers individual countries face — like how to secure funding for the infrastructure investments needed for the energy transition. 

The Santa Marta group is "really answering a lot of the questions that countries have always had on how on earth do we even start to transition our whole economy off fossil fuels?" Scott said.

This expertise being shared and tested in practice can show what such a transformation could look like and the knowledge brought into COP, added Scott. 

Few countries have announced national TAFF plans

Since the Santa Marta conference, Germany, France and the Netherlands have released national plans to phase out fossil fuels. While the plans contain no new commitments, experts see them as a positive first step. 

Kumar says more countries could announce similar plans just ahead of the second TAFF summit in Tuvalu next year. Finland presented its TAFF roadmap in the first week of COP, but analysts do not expect many more countries to follow suit soon. 

Another potential boost that could get momentum going is Brazil's recently published global roadmap to transition away from fossil fuels, promised at last year's COP.

The nearly 200-page document seeks to serve as a toolkit for countries shifting from fossil fuels to renewable energy, such as wind and solar. It envisages countries making a phased transition between 2028 and 2033.

The aim, Ana Toni, CEO of last year's COP, told AP, is to break down the transition into "smaller actionable solutions" and help countries make practical progress. 

The roadmap suggests measures tailored to each country's circumstances, including dependence on fossil fuel and financial capacities. "Levers" it proposes include reducing fossil fuel subsidies, expanding renewable energy and cutting methane emissions. 

Kumar called it a "customizable" approach that allows every country to take action, regardless of its situation. "The question really is now what countries do with it and how much momentum they can generate," she added. 

Focus on electrification 

This year's climate change summit in Turkey — taking place after a series of deadly heat waves in Europe and a glacier collapse in Nepal that killed more than 1,400 people — isn't expected to result in any big announcements about fossil fuels.

Global renewable energy capacity has surged in recent years, making up a third of the world's power generation for the first time in 2025. But many countries still rely on coal, oil and gas. 

A database compiled by the German environmental organization Urgewald shows that most oil and gas companies worldwide, among them QatarEnergy, Saudi Aramco and Russia's Gazprom, are exploring or developing new reserves.

Meanwhile, subsidies for fossil fuels amount to around $920 billion (around €830 billion) per year, according to data from the OECD, but that number is falling. 

One issue Turkey is pushing at the COP conference is electrification. It's calling on countries to endorse the goal of 35% of final energy consumption coming from electricity by 2035 — up from around 20% today.  

The pledge has drawn criticism from some circles, though, for leaving open whether that additional electricity needs to come from clean sources. 

Ideally, Kumar said, electrification would be powered by renewables and tied to a goal of phasing out polluting fossil fuels. 

"If something like that could make it to the final text, that would be a really good sign for momentum."
