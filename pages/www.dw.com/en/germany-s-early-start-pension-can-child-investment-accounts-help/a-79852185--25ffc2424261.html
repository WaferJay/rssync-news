Germany's Early Start Pension: Can child investment accounts help?

Srinivas Mazumdaru
11/23/2026November 23, 2026

Germany plans to pay €10 a month into investment accounts for children to encourage long-term retirement saving. Economists say the measure may help build wealth but won't solve the country's pension funding challenge.

Germany's pension system is under growing strain as demographic change leaves a shrinking workforce funding the pensions of an increasing number of retirees.  

In the 1960s, for instance, six workers contributed to the pay-as-you-go public retirement system for each pensioner. Today, that figure has fallen to just two contributors per retiree.

According to the Federal Statistical Office, Germany's fertility rate is currently 1.32 children per woman,and has remained below the 2.1 needed to maintain a stable population since the 1970s.

As a large cohort of baby boomers reaches retirement age, fewer young people are entering the workforce. Germany's working-age population is shrinking by around 1% each year due to demographic change alone, according to the German bank LBBW.

To keep up, nearly a quarter of the federal budget, over €123 billion ($135 billion), is transferred to the statutory pension system each year to help finance pension payments.

Now to help ease the pressure on the pension system, Chancellor Friedrich Merz's government plans to implement a raft of reforms, including a supplementary, capital-funded savings scheme known as the Frühstartrente, or Early Start Pension.

How does the Early Start Pension work?

Under the plan, the state would pay €10 per month into an investment account for every child and teenager aged six to 18.

Beneficiaries would not be able to access the money until they reach retirement age, though current plans would allow the funds to be transferred into private retirement accounts. Investment returns would be tax-free during the accumulation phase and taxed only when funds are withdrawn.

Assuming an average annual return of 7%, the €1,440 contributed by the government over 12 years would grow to around €53,000 by retirement, according to government projections.

Parents and relatives would be able to make additional contributions. If they invest an extra €50 per month, the account could grow to as much as €300,000 by retirement. 

Starting to save early makes sense because of the power of compound interest, Axel Börsch-Supan, a professor of economics at the Technical University of Munich, told DW.

"But it will not solve Germany's pension problems, which stem from the pay-as-you-go system and costly policies such as the pension level guarantee and retirement at 63," he added, referring to a provision that allows workers with at least 45 years of pension contributions to retire at age 63 with a full pension.

What are the German government's pension, labor reforms?

Börsch-Supan argues that government subsidies under the program are likely to benefit higher-income families more than lower-income households.

"That's a waste of taxpayer money, because higher-income individuals are already well-positioned to provide for their own retirement and that of their children," he said.

The Ifo Institute, one of Germany's leading economic think tanks, shares a similar assessment.

It argues that the scheme effectively means "the government hands out money indiscriminately, only to reclaim it through taxes from the same citizens."

What does the government say?

The government, however, argues that the plan gives every child a head start in the capital markets regardless of their family's financial circumstances and helps them build retirement savings and long-term wealth.

Similar capital market-based savings and investment accounts for children exist in several countries, including the United Kingdom, Israel, Canada and Singapore.

Many of these programs include government contributions, matching payments or tax incentives designed to encourage long-term saving and asset accumulation from an early age.

But Germany's plan differs slightly as it links state-funded childhood investment accounts directly to retirement provision.

The government also hopes the program will boost financial literacy among children and young adults by familiarizing them with investing, saving and capital markets.

What Germans young and old think about the pension system

Can the scheme encourage long-term investing?

Johannes Geyer, deputy head of the department of public economics at the German Institute for Economic Research (DIW Berlin), says there is little evidence the Early Start Pension will fundamentally change how people engage with financial markets.

"The idea is that people will develop a connection to the capital markets through their experiences with their investment portfolios," he told DW. "But this is not very convincing. We neither know what experiences the future will bring nor what conclusions people will draw from them."

Geyer stresses that the program's impact will not be visible for decades because participants cannot access the funds until retirement.

"If we start now with the 2020 and 2021 cohorts, we're talking about the 2080s. That's a bit too far in the future to be able to draw meaningful conclusions."

In the meantime, he says, the scheme's most immediate effect may be to strengthen ties between families and financial institutions, particularly if account holders make additional contributions.

Opposition parties, including the Greens and the Left Party, have criticized the plan, arguing it would reinforce social disparities and boost inequality since better-off parents could pay more into accounts.

Yet Geyer says the measure won't increase inequality per se. "Sure, wealthy families are more likely to top up their contributions more substantially. But they probably would have done that anyway," he underscored.

"A more interesting question is whether the measure can also encourage people with limited financial means to invest in stocks," he said. 

What else should be done to stabilize the pension system?

Geyer points out that Germany cannot entirely escape the costs of demographic decline and an aging society.

To address the challenge, he calls for a moderate increase in pension contributions, a broader contributor base that includes groups such as the self-employed, and policies aimed at attracting more qualified workers to the labor market.

Börsch-Supan, who specializes in the economics of aging, says the government's primary goal should be to stabilize the pay-as-you-go statutory pension system.

"A transition to a fully funded pension system would actually be desirable, but it would initially place a heavy double burden on the younger generation, because they would have to save for themselves while also paying contributions for their parents," he explained.

The expert also suggests other measures like restoring the sustainability factor, which is designed to link annual pension hikes to demographic shifts, and linking the retirement age to life expectancy.
