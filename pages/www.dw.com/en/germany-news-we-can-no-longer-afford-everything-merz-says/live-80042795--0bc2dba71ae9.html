Left Party lawmakers 'blockade' former Mossad boss Barnea, may face reprimand

Mark Hallam

Two socialist Left Party lawmakers in the Bundestag say they briefly obstructed a meeting involving the former head of Israeli intelligence agency Mossad, David Barnea, at parliament in Berlin on Wednesday.

Desiree Becker said that she and her colleague Tamara Mazzi were the two women visible on a video that initially spread online seated or crouched in front of the door to the area where Barnea was set to appear.

Becker told the dpa news agency she was protesting because the Bundestag should not play host to war criminals; she designated the 2024 pager attack on Hezbollah by Israel, which Barnea says he had a role in, as a war crime. 

People who attended the meeting said the disruption only lasted around one minute. 

The Bundestag said later on Wednesday that parliament speaker Julia Klöckner would be referring the incident to parliament's Council of Elders, or Ältestenrat, that supports the speaker and is supposed to help support the smooth working of parliament. 

The parliament said that federal police had been at the scene and that the details were still under investigation. 

Becker of the Left Party said she experienced no problems from police amid the demonstration, saying "we were only holding up pictures." She said the pictures showed people killed in the detonation of explosives built into Hezbollah's communciation devices.

Omid Nouripour, one of the parliament's deputy speakers, criticzed the lawmakers' action in comments to Der Spiegel. He said that impeding the work of other lawmakers was not covered by their public mandate but was rather simple activism. Nouripour added that many things that other parties did were not to his personal taste, but "I have to lodge my objections in parliament with words."

In a separate incident, a man was forcibly removed from the meeting by police. He shouted a mixture of German and English at Barnea, calling him a war criminal. Then, he urged the police to arrest Barnea instead of him.

'Germany's first supermodel' Vera Lehndorff dies aged 87

Mark Hallam

Vera Anna Gottliebe Gräfin von Lehndorff, better known simply as "Veruschka" or as Vera Lehndorff in her heyday in the 1960s and 70s, has died aged 87.

An actor, painter and photographer, as well as the daughter of one of the officers who was executed for his role in a failed attempt to assassinate Adolf Hitler in 1944, Veruschka was probably best known as "Germany's first supermodel." (Not to be confused with Germany's Next Top Model.)

Born to Prussian nobility, she and her mother were left homeless and destitute after the war. As a young woman she moved to Italy to study design and later to work as a painter, before being discovered as a model in the 1960s.

Standing 1 meter 90 (more than 6 foot 2 inches) tall, Versuchka became internationally famous in the fashion scene, not least thanks to her role in the 1966 cult film "Blow Up" and later in the 1971 film "Veruschka — the poetry of a woman" by fashion photographer Franco Rubartelli, her first lover. For a time she was thought to be the world's best paid model. 

She made headlines in 1971 when she took part in Alice Schwarzer's "We have had an abortion" campaign designed to raise awareness about a process that was illegal in Germany at the time. 

Veruschka continued her work as a painter for decades and also occasionally returned to modeling and acting roles, including playing a German member of the nobility, Gräfin von Wallenstein, in the poker game in the 2006 James Bond film "Casino Royale."

She was also the subject of the 2004 documentary film about her life, "Veruschka — A Life for the Camera." Her autobiography was published a few years later.

Bayern Munich say Musiala first exhibited neurological symptoms last April

Mark Hallam

Bayern Munich says it has held talks with midfielder Jamal Musiala's management amid the difficulties surrounding his neurological symptoms and after comments from club management about his lucrative 2025 contract extension prompted criticism and controversy (read the backstory from Jon Harding on our sports desk here). 

"A constructive discussion took place yesterday, Tuesday, between FC Bayern Munich and Jamal Musiala's management," the club said in a statement. "For both parties, the player's health is the absolute priority. Jamal Musiala will receive full support throuhgout his recovery process."

The club also clarified when Musiala — who collapsed twice in pre-season friendlies as a result of what he has described as "absence seizures" — first started exhibiting neurological symptoms that only became widely known with the announcement of his diagnosis at the start of this season. 

"In light of various rumors circulating over the past few days, FC Bayern and 11WINS — the agency representing Jamal — wish to clarify the following: Jamal Musiala was first examined for absence-like symptoms on April 22, 2025," the club said. "This examination took place in consultation with FC Bayern and the club's medical department, immediately following the onset of these neurological symptoms." 

Musiala had signed a contract extension to 2030 earlier that year. Several senior Bayern officials had voiced doubts about his contractual status in recent days, saying it appeared unlikely he would be able to return to his past peak form going forward. This prompted a stern rebuke from the players' union and others. 

After being sidelined with a hamstring injury in September, Musiala returned to action last week in the 1-1 draw with Cologne.

AfD in Saxony-Anhalt put forward Siegmund to head state government

Mark Hallam

The right-wing populist Alternative for Germany (AfD) has formally put forward its leader in Saxony-Anhalt to head the eastern state's next regional government.

Given the stalemate after September's elections, with the AfD by far the largest party but still well short of a majority in its own right, it's anything by clear that Siegmund will receive the 42 votes he'd need to become state premier.

The AfD holds 39 seats in the Magdeburg parliament so Siegmund would need three votes from members of other parties in the secret ballot. 

But what's now clear is that a vote to see if he can command a majority will go ahead, provisionally on December 8. Siegmund had said last week that he planned to take this step. 

"I am working on the assumption that there will only be one vote," Siegmund said, voicing confidence in winning the required support, even though nobody outside his party has indicated an intention to vote for him. 

The left-wing populist BSW, which holds five seats, has said its members intend to abstain, while 14 of 15 CDU lawmakers in the state have said they intend to leave the chamber in the first two rounds of voting.

How does the election process work in such situations where a majority vote seems unlikely? 

It's not clear how the process might end as yet.

But if a candidate stands in two votes in the state parliament — which must take place within one week of each other — and cannot command a majority in either, a new vote is triggered on whether to call new elections. This must take place within two weeks. In this situation, a simple majority voting in favor would suffice to trigger another election.

If there is no majority vote for another election, then a third vote for a state government leader is triggered.

In this final round, a candidate only needs a majority of the total votes cast, not a majority of the total number of members of the chamber, in order to be elected. 

In the event that even this third vote is not decisive, with a tie of votes for and against — which given the arithmetic in the parliament does seem at least to be a conceivable possibility — then Germany's written laws don't make it explicitly clear what the tie-break function would be.

Legal experts have said they believe the most likely solution would be to continue holding votes until a majority or plurality decision can be reached.

Wadephul: Reduced Foreign Ministry budget 'difficult to understand' given global tensions

Mark Hallam

German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul has voiced dissatisfaction with cuts to his department's 2027 funding during Wednesday's Bundestag budget debate. 

He described the planned budget for his department as "responsible," albeit adding: "But to be honest, I cannot give my budget a much better evaluation than that."

Wadephul said that given the precarious geopolitical situation it was "difficult to understand" why the Foreign Ministry was not exempt from the more general savings drive.

He noted that while the Defense Ministry budget was bolstered to a large extent by the so-called "special fund" to boost defense and rearmament spending, "not a single euro cent" of that extra-budgetary financial allowance flowed to the Foreign Ministry. 

The Foreign Ministry's proposed 2027 budget is smaller than this year's, but not by very much. The proposals foresee cutting it from €6.02 billion (roughly $6.58 billion) to €5.99 billion. The largest planned cut is in contributions to the UN and the international community, falling by roughly €200 million or almost a quarter of the 2026 total.

Authorities in Nuremberg defuse World War II bomb

Mark Hallam

Experts in Nuremberg have successfully defused a World War II bomb not far south of the city center.

The 225-kilo bomb was rendered inactive just before 2 p.m. local time (1300 GMT/UTC), city authorities said in a press release. 

The US bomb dropped in an air raid had been unearthed a day earlier at a construction site. 

A precautionary evacuation radius of 500 meters was established to deal with the explosive, with roughly 700 residents asked to leave their homes in the hours before the operation. 

"All those affected by the evacuation can now return to their homes," the city wrote. 

An affected bus route was also resuming normal service, and authorities said that road closures and diversions were also being lifted. 

Around 250 personnel were deployed for the operation. 

Discoveries of unexploded ordnance from World War II are commonplace to this day in Germany. However, Nuremberg in the north of Bavaria represents a site rather further south and inland than the most common locations, which tend to be in the north and west, in easier reach of Allied bombing raids often launched from the UK.

The heavily industrialized western Ruhr Valley region and major cities like Cologne in North Rhine-Westphalia are the most common sites for such finds.

Berlin unveils memorial to Holocaust survivor Margot Friedländer

The city of Berlin unveiled a new memorial to Holocaust survivor Margot Friedländer.

A sign explaining the history of a building in Berlin where Margot Friedländer, then still known as Margot Bendheim, was forced to live by the Nazis has now been established.

The plaque was unveiled in Berlin-Kreuzberg by Green district Mayor Clara Herrmann.

It explains how Jews, including Margot, her mother and her brother, were forced to live there. The building on Skalitzer Strasse also housed forced laborers.

Margot's family was detained and sent to Auschwitz in 1943, where they were murdered. Margot escaped but was eventually sent to the Theresienstadt concentration camp.

She survived the war, after which she moved to the United States, but returned in 2009 to her former home. Up until her death last year, she was engaged in educating people about the Holocaust.

CDU defends budget drafted in face of 'challenges'

The head of the CDU lawmakers in the Bundestag, Thorstein Frei, defended the government's 2027 budget, despite it being "full of challenges."

He said that taxation had been eased where it most benefited people and police, intelligence agencies and the military have been strengthened.

He acknowledged that the coalition "is not yet where it wants to be" and is also not blind to the fact part of the economic growth is coming from public spending, but he pointed to positive signals from the economy, such as the increasing business climate index.

Regarding the massive increase in military spending, Frei said: "We want to be strong so that, in the end, we won't have to use that strength."

Left Party slams militarization and cuts in budget

Heidi Reichinnek, the head of the socialist Left Party in the Bundestag, has also commented on the government's budget, accusing the coalition of drafting a budget "at the expense of the majority."

"Militarization and unbridled arms buildup on the one hand, brutal social cutbacks and a spree of budget cuts on the other," Reichinnek said.

She accused the government of trying to protect the profits of the wealthy and big corporations while telling everyone else to "tighten the belt."

The left-wing lawmaker said a windfall tax on electricity companies would have helped people without leaving a gap in the budget, as happened with the electricity tax reduction that the coalition agreed on.

Merz defends support for Ukraine in budget

Germany has been Ukraine's biggest backer in Europe, incurring costs to help defend against the Russian invasion.

Despite falling support among German voters for this policy and increasing budget difficulties, Chancellor Merz defended the government's decision to keep funding Kyiv.

"Russia wants more than Ukraine," Merz said in response to opposition parties during the 2027 budget debate.

"Anyone who fails to grasp this does not understand this world, or is pursuing entirely different aims with regard to our country's freedom, openness and liberalism," he added.

He also pointed to incidents within Germany that have been attributed to Russia, including the discovery of an explosive-laden drone at Leipzig Airport in August.

11/25/2026November 25, 2026

'We no longer have the money' Merz says in budget debate

Chancellor Friedrich Merz has spoken in the Bundestag as the debate opens on the government's budget plans for 2027.

"We no longer have the money for everything," Merz said in his remarks to the German parliament.

He described the tight budget, which has to be finalized this week, as an "accurate reflection" of the "difficult situation as a country — geopolitically, technologically, and demographically" that Germany finds itself in.

"We will no longer be able to afford everything with tax revenue that we have become all too accustomed to for far too long," Merz added.

The government has €560.9 billion (around $614 billion) to spend in its core budget for 2027, but both Merz's conservative CDU/CSU and the Social Democrats, who make up the coalition government, have stressed that the budget for 2028 will be even more challenging.

The extra borrowing for defense and infrastructure, that marked a break with years of debt avoidance, "is limiting our room to maneuver in the coming years to an extent that many have not yet truly come to terms with," Merz warned.
