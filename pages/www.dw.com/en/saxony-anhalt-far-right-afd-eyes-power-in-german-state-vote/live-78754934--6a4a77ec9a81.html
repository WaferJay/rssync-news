What you need to know

  • Saxony-Anhalt voters are set to elect a new state parliament in a vote being closely watched across Germany

WATCH: Saxony-Anhalt State Premier and CDU lead candidate Sven Schulze warns against AfD taking power

Saxony-Anhalt State Premier Sven Schulze is determined to keep the AfD from becoming the first far-right party since World War II to lead a German state government.

In the run-up to today's vote, he repeated his campaign message "It's far from decided," over and over.

The party is polling at over 40% compared to Schulze's center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU) at around 23%.

Schulze is hoping the AfD will stay below 50% and so be unable to secure a majority in the state parliament, and with no other party willing to join it in a coalition, the AfD would be unable to form a government.

That would leave it up to Schulze to form a coalition, perhaps with as many as three partners, and remain in office.

Saxony-Anhalt morning turnout sharply higher than in 2021

Voter turnout in Saxony-Anhalt's state election was markedly higher by midday than at the same point five years ago.

By that time, 34.7% of eligible voters had cast their ballots at polling stations, according to the state returning officer. At the same time in the 2021 election, turnout stood at 22.4%.

Final turnout in 2021 was 60.3%, including postal votes.

The sharply higher midday turnout points to strong voter mobilization, but it is too early to say which parties are benefiting,

Nearly 1.7 million people aged 18 and over are eligible to vote in Sunday's election.

The state's 2,130 polling stations remain open until 6 p.m., and postal ballots can also be submitted until then.

The state returning officer said voting had proceeded smoothly during the morning, with no unusually long waiting times reported.

WATCH: AfD's Ulrich Siegmund tells DW he wants to make history

The Alternative for Germany (AfD) lead candidate Ulrich Siegmund aims to turn his small state into a blueprint for right-wing politics across Germany.

The AfD's "100-day immediate action plan" contains a key promise to "deport from minute one" foreign nationals who have no legal right to stay but whose presence is tolerated for humanitarian reasons. Many have found work and established their lives in Germany.

The action plan also calls for cutting funding for pro-democracy NGOs, requiring refugees to work and prioritizing a love for the German homeland over social diversity.

Migration debate looms large in Saxony-Anhalt despite low foreign population

Migration was one of the major campaign issues in Saxony-Anhalt.

This is despite the fact that, compared with other German states, relatively few non-Germans live there.

At the end of 2025, foreign nationals accounted for around 9% of Saxony-Anhalt's population, roughly half the share of North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany's most populous state.

The claim that "foreigners are living off taxpayers" is also misleading. Compared with other German states, Saxony-Anhalt has a relatively low proportion of foreign nationals among potential recipients of welfare benefits such as basic income support.

Although Saxony-Anhalt is one of Germany's economically weaker states, its population has grown in recent years, according to migration figures.

Since 2016, more non-Germans have moved to Saxony-Anhalt than have moved away. The net migration balance for German citizens has also been positive for the past six years.

Polls open in Saxony-Anhalt state election

Polling stations have opened in the eastern German state of Saxony-Anhalt, where voters are choosing a new state parliament.

Around 1.7 million people aged 18 and over are eligible to vote. Polling stations opened at 8 a.m. local time (0600 GMT/UTC) and will close at 6 p.m., when counting begins.

Voters are electing the members of the new state parliament in Magdeburg.

They can cast two votes: one for a direct constituency candidate and one for a political party.

The new parliament will subsequently elect the state premier.

The state returning officer expects a preliminary final result in the early hours of Monday morning.

ANALYSIS: Why is this election so closely watched?

Matthew Moore Political correspondent

I have not experienced a more polarizing election since Brexit or Trump vs. Hillary Clinton in 2016. That is because the stakes are so high.

For a number of reasons, the decision voters in Saxony-Anhalt reach today is being watched closely in Berlin and beyond:

  • Watershed moment: Should the AfD secure an absolute majority, it would be the first time a right-wing populist party has won power in a state in Germany in the postwar era.
  • Firewall: Should the AfD not secure an absolute majority, the focus will turn to the conservative CDU. Pressure will mount on the party to rethink its longstanding policy of not governing with or passing laws with the far-right AfD or far-left Left Party. Such a debate risks splitting the CDU.
  • Chancellor: A dire result for the conservatives may lead to calls for the deeply unpopular Chancellor Friedrich Merz to be replaced. In CDU circles in Berlin, the talk is of an election that could 'change the republic.'

Saxony-Anhalt heads to the polls as coalition math takes center stage

Saxony-Anhalt, one of Germany's 16 federal states, boasts a rich cultural and historical heritage.
 
Home to around 2.1 million people, it is one of Germany's most sparsely populated states. Only two cities, the state capital Magdeburg, and the city of Halle, have more than 200,000 inhabitants.
 
Today, voters will elect a new state parliament.

The center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU) has led the state government since 2002 as the leading party in governing coalitions. But the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) has dominated polls in the run-up to the vote, typically drawing support in the 40% range, well beyond the CDU's low-to-mid-20% range.

If polling holds, the AfD would become Saxony-Anhalt's largest party for the first time. Every other major party has ruled out allying with it, meaning it would likely need to secure an outright majority in order to govern. A broader coalition made up of other parties is the other potential outcome.
 
A key thing to watch when exit polls and preliminary results are released tonight is whether smaller parties fall below the 5% threshold and lose representation.
 
Under election law, parties must obtain at least 5% of party-list votes (known as second votes) to secure representation.
 
This would mean more seats in the state parliament for the remaining parties, including the AfD, on a proportional basis. That could allow the AfD to secure an outright majority if enough smaller parties fail to clear the threshold.
